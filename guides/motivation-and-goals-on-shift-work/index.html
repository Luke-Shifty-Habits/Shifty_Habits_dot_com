Staying motivated, and making time for goals, when you work shifts

After a twelve-hour shift, “just find the motivation” is useless advice. Motivation is the first thing shift work takes from you. The people who keep making progress aren't more motivated — they've arranged things so they don't need to be.

By Luke — shift worker, and the developer of Shifty Habits

The short answer: don't rely on motivation after a shift — you won't have any. Decide in advance, and shrink each habit to a version you'd do even exhausted. Put personal goals on specific days of your roster, usually the middle of your days off. And set goals per roster cycle ("six sessions this rotation") instead of per week, so a changing roster doesn't break them.

Staying motivated when you're exhausted after work

Being exhausted after a shift isn't a character flaw, and it isn't laziness. Long hours, broken sleep and physically or emotionally heavy work genuinely drain you, and the part of your day you have left is the worst part. Planning to rely on motivation then is planning to fail.

What works instead:

  • Decide before the shift, not after. "When I get home I'll walk round the block" is a decision you make at 06:00 with a clear head. At 19:00 you just follow it.
  • Shrink it. Every habit needs a version you'd do on your worst day — five minutes, one page, one set. The point isn't progress, it's not breaking the chain of doing it.
  • Remove the friction. Kit by the door, book on the pillow, food already made. Every step you take out is one less thing for a tired brain to argue with.
  • Be honest about rest. Some evenings the right answer really is nothing. Decide that in advance too, so it's a plan and not a failure.
Motivation follows action more often than it leads it. Start small enough that you don't need it.

Making time for personal goals on shifts

Shift work doesn't leave less free time than a nine-to-five, usually. It leaves it in strange shapes — four days in a row, then nothing for a week. The trick is to use the shape rather than fight it:

  • Look at your roster, not your week. Find the days that are genuinely yours. On most rosters that's the middle of a run of days off — not the first (recovery) and not the last (getting ready).
  • Book those days in advance. Put the goal — the course, the project, the long run — on specific days in your roster, the way you'd book an appointment.
  • Keep a small daily thread on work days. Ten minutes of language practice, a page of writing. Enough to stay connected to the goal, small enough to survive a shift.
  • Use the in-between time. A commute, a quiet break on a night shift. Not for the hard thinking, but for reading, listening and planning.

Setting goals when your roster keeps changing

Goals written as "every Monday" or "three times a week" assume the week is stable. On a rotating roster it isn't, and a goal that breaks every time the roster moves is a goal you'll abandon.

Write goals in the unit your life actually repeats in:

  • On a repeating cycle, set goals per cycle: "six training sessions every eight days" on a 4-on-4-off, rather than "three a week".
  • On a roster that changes, set goals when you get the roster. Look at the next block, count the days that are genuinely free, and set the goal for that block.
  • Attach habits to shift types, not dates, so the daily part of the goal follows the roster without being rescheduled.
  • Review at the end of each cycle, not each week. A week only shows you part of the pattern.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stay motivated when I'm exhausted after work?

Don't rely on motivation after a shift. Decide what you'll do before the shift, when you have a clear head, and shrink each habit to a version you'd manage even exhausted — five minutes, one page, a walk round the block. Remove friction in advance, and plan some evenings as genuine rest so they aren't failures.

How do I make time for personal goals while working shifts?

Use the shape of your roster. Book the goal onto specific days that are genuinely yours, usually the middle of a run of days off rather than the first or last, and keep a small daily thread — ten minutes — going on work days so you stay connected to it.

How do I set goals when my roster keeps changing?

Set goals in the unit your schedule actually repeats in. On a repeating cycle, set them per cycle, such as six sessions every eight days. On a roster that changes, set them for each block when you receive it, based on the days that are genuinely free, and review at the end of each cycle rather than each week.

Is it normal to have no energy for habits after a shift?

Yes. Long hours, broken sleep and demanding work genuinely drain you, so having nothing left after a shift is normal rather than a lack of discipline. Plan for it with smaller versions of your habits on work days and save the bigger efforts for days off.
