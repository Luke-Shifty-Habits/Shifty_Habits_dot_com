How to manage your sleep routine when working shifts

On shift work, sleep isn't something that happens at the end of the day — it's something you have to schedule, defend and set up for, differently on every type of shift. Get it right and every other habit gets easier.

By Luke — shift worker, and the developer of Shifty Habits

The short answer: treat sleep as an appointment you plan for each shift type, not something that happens when you get round to it. Decide in advance when you sleep on earlies, lates, nights and days off, make the room dark, cool and quiet whatever the time, and run the same short wind-down before every sleep so your body learns the cue rather than the clock.

Most adults need at least seven hours in every 24. On shift work it doesn't have to come in one piece, but it does have to come.

Plan sleep per shift type

A single "bedtime" can't work on a rotating roster. What can work is a sleep plan for each kind of shift you do, decided once and then reused every time that shift comes round.

Set the room up for daytime sleep

Your body expects to be awake in daylight and asleep in the dark, so a daytime sleep starts at a disadvantage. The room has to make up for it:

  • Dark. Blackout blinds or curtains, or an eye mask. Even a little light around the edges makes sleep lighter.
  • Cool. Bedrooms warm up during the day. A fan helps, and doubles as steady background noise.
  • Quiet. Earplugs or white noise for the deliveries, lawnmowers and school runs a night sleeper has to sleep through.
  • Defended. Tell the people you live with when you're sleeping, and make the phone silent — without making yourself unreachable if work genuinely needs you.

A wind-down that works at any time

The point of a wind-down routine is that the steps become the cue for sleep. That only works if they're the same every time, whether it's 22:00 before an early or 08:00 after a night. Keep it short — ten to fifteen minutes — and portable:

  • Lights down (and sunglasses on the drive home after nights)
  • Phone on silent or into its sleep mode, and out of reach
  • The same two or three steps every time: shower, teeth, a few pages of a book

If you finish work wired, add a buffer before the wind-down — a walk, a meal, twenty minutes of something dull — rather than going straight from handover to bed.

Naps, caffeine and rotation

Naps are a tool, not a failure. A short nap before a night shift can make the early hours of the shift easier. Keep naps either short (around twenty minutes) or long enough to be a proper sleep, and keep them well away from your main sleep.

Caffeine lasts much longer than it feels like it does. Front-load it at the start of the shift and stop several hours before you plan to sleep — on nights, around the halfway point is a sensible cut-off.

Rotation direction matters too. Rotating forward (days, then lates, then nights) is generally easier on sleep than rotating backwards, because it's easier to stay up later than to go to bed earlier. You probably don't choose your roster, but the rotation patterns guide explains what yours is doing to you.

When to get help

Some disruption is normal on shift work. Persistent trouble sleeping even when you have the chance, or sleepiness that makes driving home feel dangerous, isn't something to push through. Talk to a doctor — shift work sleep problems are well recognised and there is help available.

Track sleep like any other habit

Rather than tracking hours, track the behaviour you control: "in bed within an hour of getting home" on night shifts, "lights out by 21:30" before earlies, "wind-down routine" every day. Attach each one to the shift types it belongs to, and you'll see quickly which shift your sleep falls apart on.

Frequently asked questions

How do I manage my sleep routine when working shifts?

Plan sleep for each shift type in advance — earlies, lates, nights and days off — instead of trying to keep one bedtime. Make the bedroom dark, cool and quiet whatever the time, and run the same short wind-down before every sleep so the routine, not the clock, becomes your cue for sleep.

How much sleep do shift workers need?

The same as anyone else: most adults need at least seven hours in every 24. On shift work it doesn't have to come in one block — a main sleep plus a nap can make it up — but the total still has to be there, and running short across a block of shifts builds a debt that takes days to repay.

How do I sleep during the day after a night shift?

Go to bed as soon as you can after getting home, keep daylight out of your eyes on the commute, and sleep in a room that is properly dark, cool and quiet, using blackout blinds, earplugs or white noise. Stop caffeine around the middle of the shift, and tell the people you live with when you're sleeping.

Should I nap before a night shift?

A nap before a night shift can make the early hours easier for many people. Keep it either short, around twenty minutes, or long enough to be a proper sleep, and finish it with enough time to wake up properly before you start work.

How can I keep my phone quiet while I sleep but still get called in to work?

Use a Do Not Disturb mode that lets chosen contacts and repeat callers through.
